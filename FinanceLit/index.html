Study Notes — Budgeting

Key concepts for understanding budgeting and personal finance.

Key Concepts

  • Gross Income vs. Net IncomeGross income is your total pay before any deductions. Net income is what you actually take home after taxes, CPP contributions, and Employment Insurance (EI) are deducted. Budgeting should always be based on net income, not gross.
  • The 50/30/20 RuleA widely used budgeting guideline: allocate 50% of net income to Needs (rent, groceries, utilities), 30% to Wants (dining out, entertainment, subscriptions), and 20% to Savings or Debt Repayment.
    \(\text{Needs} = \frac{\text{Net}}{12} \times 0.50 \qquad \text{Wants} = \frac{\text{Net}}{12} \times 0.30 \qquad \text{Savings} = \frac{\text{Net}}{12} \times 0.20\)
  • Fixed vs. Variable ExpensesFixed expenses stay the same each month (rent, car payment, insurance). Variable expenses change month to month (groceries, gas, entertainment). Budgeting both is important because variable expenses are easy to overspend on.
  • Why Start with Monthly Budgeting?Annual income is converted to monthly because most bills (rent, phone, utilities) are billed monthly. Dividing your annual net income by 12 gives your monthly spending baseline.
  • Emergency FundFinancial experts recommend saving 3–6 months of living expenses in an emergency fund before investing. This is funded from the savings portion (20%) of your budget.
  • Deductions from Gross Pay (Canadian Context)In Canada, deductions include federal and provincial income tax (varies by bracket), Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions (5.70% of insurable earnings in 2024), and Employment Insurance (EI) premiums.

Study Notes — Rates & Percentages

Key concepts for calculating percentage change and unit rates.

Key Concepts

  • Percentage ChangeMeasures how much a quantity has increased or decreased relative to its original value, expressed as a percentage. A positive result = increase; negative = decrease.
    \(\% \text{ Change} = \dfrac{\text{New} - \text{Old}}{\text{Old}} \times 100\)
  • Percent Increase vs. Percent DecreaseIf the new value is greater than the original, the result is a percent increase. If the new value is smaller, it is a percent decrease. Both use the same formula — the sign of the result tells you which it is.
  • Unit RateA ratio that compares a quantity to exactly 1 unit. Used to determine the cost per item, speed, or any per-unit comparison. Essential for comparing value between differently sized products.
    \(\text{Unit Rate} = \dfrac{\text{Total Cost}}{\text{Quantity}}\)
  • Best Buy ComparisonTo determine which product offers better value, calculate the unit rate for each and compare. The lower unit rate is the better value — this works regardless of package size or price difference.
  • Markup and DiscountA markup increases the original price by a percentage. A discount reduces the price. Both are calculated using percentage change.
    \(\text{Sale Price} = P \times (1 - d) \qquad \text{Marked Up Price} = P \times (1 + m)\)
  • Rate vs. RatioA ratio compares two quantities of the same unit (e.g., 3:5). A rate compares two quantities of different units (e.g., 80 km/h, $2.50/L).

Study Notes — Appreciation & Depreciation

Key concepts for understanding how assets change in value over time.

Key Concepts

  • AppreciationWhen an asset increases in value over time, it is said to appreciate. Common examples include real estate, land, stocks, and collectibles.
    \(FV = PV \times (1 + r)^{t}\)
  • DepreciationWhen an asset decreases in value over time, it depreciates. This is common with vehicles, electronics, and machinery.
    \(FV = PV \times (1 - r)^{t}\)
  • Why Exponential, Not Linear?Depreciation and appreciation apply the rate to the current value each year — not the original. A car depreciating at 15%/year loses more money in year 1 than in year 5 because the base value is smaller.
  • Variables ExplainedPV = Present Value (starting value), FV = Future Value, r = annual rate as a decimal (e.g., 8% → 0.08), t = time in years. The exponent t makes this compound change rather than simple.
  • Common Appreciation AssetsReal estate in growing cities (avg. 3–7%/year), stocks and ETFs (avg. 7–10%/year historically), precious metals (gold, silver), and collectibles.
  • Common Depreciation AssetsNew cars lose roughly 15–20% in year 1 alone and can lose 50–60% in the first 5 years. Electronics, appliances, and furniture also depreciate quickly.

Study Notes — Interest & Borrowing

Key concepts for understanding how interest works in savings and debt.

Key Concepts

  • Simple InterestInterest calculated only on the original principal — it does not compound. Results in linear growth. Used in some short-term loans, bonds, and GICs.
    \(I = P \times r \times t \qquad \text{Total} = P + I\)
  • Compound InterestInterest is calculated on the principal AND on previously earned interest. This causes exponential growth and is used by most banks, credit cards, mortgages, and investment accounts.
    \(A = P \times \left(1 + \dfrac{r}{n}\right)^{n \cdot t}\)
  • Compounding Frequency (n)How often interest is added to the principal. More frequent compounding = higher effective return or cost. Annual (n=1), Monthly (n=12), and Daily (n=365) are the most common.
  • The Rule of 72A quick shortcut: divide 72 by the annual interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money.
    \(\text{Years to Double} \approx \dfrac{72}{r}\)
  • Principal (P)The original amount of money invested or borrowed. Making extra payments on principal reduces the total interest paid dramatically.
  • Interest in Debt vs. SavingsCompound interest works for you in savings. But it works against you in debt — credit card balances can double fast because unpaid interest is added to the balance.
